Working papers

Water Conservation in the Classroom: A Randomized Comparison of a Serious Game and an Interactive Lecture

With Leonardo Boncinelli.

Read abstract

Educational interventions can promote sustainable resource use by improving environmental knowledge and encouraging behavioral change. Yet, evidence on the relative effectiveness of alternative educational approaches remains limited. This paper contributes to the literature on environmental and economic education by comparing two classroom-based approaches to teaching water conservation: a novel serious game and a content-equivalent interactive lecture. We evaluate their differential effects on environmental literacy among primary school children, focusing on knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported behavior. We conducted a randomized controlled trial involving approximately 220 fourth- and fifth-grade students in three primary schools in Turin, Italy. Classes were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: a game-based intervention, an interactive lecture, or a pure control group. Outcomes were measured through pre- and post-treatment surveys and a two-week follow-up. Both the game and the lecture produced substantial positive estimates for students’ knowledge relative to the control group, with no robust differences between the two active treatments. Given the small number of randomized clusters, however, wild-cluster bootstrap inference is imprecise. Behavioral outcomes were less conclusive: the game produced more favorable changes than the lecture in the aggregate self-reported water-conservation behavior index, but this difference did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple testing and wild-cluster inference. We also find no robust evidence of treatment-induced changes in attitudes. Students reported similarly high levels of engagement in both interventions. These findings suggest that both instructional formats can support environmental learning, while providing suggestive evidence of a more favorable short-run behavioral response to the game.

Eating Nature: Revealing the Ecological Cost of Food Trade through a Global Biodiversity-Risk Footprint

Ecological Economics (2026), 10.1016/j.ecolecon.2026.109142

With Silvana Dalmazzone, Giorgio Vacchiano and Marco M. Bagliani.

They talk about it on: Otto (UniTo)

Read abstract

International trade in food commodities is a major driver of global biodiversity loss through the conversion of forests to agriculture. Yet conventional deforestation metrics often fail to capture the full ecological consequences. We introduce a Biodiversity Risk indicator that weights deforested hectares by species vulnerability and endemism, offering a more nuanced assessment of biodiversity impacts from agricultural expansion into forests. Linking spatially explicit deforestation data with food trade flows, we uncover strong mismatches between countries’ Deforestation Footprint and Biodiversity Risk. Indonesia, despite lower deforestation volumes than Brazil, emerges as the most impacted country due to high species vulnerability and specific crop conversion patterns. Commodity-level analysis distinguishes locally consumed crops (e.g., meat, cassava, rice) from globally traded biodiversity-intensive commodities (e.g., palm oil, soy, natural rubber), offering new insights for targeted interventions. Our findings underscore the need to integrate biodiversity metrics into supply-chain governance and deforestation policies to promote more ecologically responsible trade.
